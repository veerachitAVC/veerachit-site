Headcount Is No Longer the Unit

Someone asked me last month how many people work at Avery AI. I gave a number. Then I spent the drive home realising the number was useless.

Not wrong. Useless. It described what the company costs. It said nothing about what the company can do.

What the number used to mean

For most of my twenty years, headcount was a reliable proxy for four things at once: capacity, revenue, seriousness, and ambition. A forty-person agency could take work a six-person agency could not. When someone told me their team size, I knew roughly what they could deliver and roughly what they were charging.

That single number did a lot of work. It is not doing it any more.

Where it broke

Month-end reporting at Claypot King used to take one person three days. Pull the POS numbers per branch, reconcile against the ERP, chase the branches that had not closed their counts, rebuild the deck. Three days, every month, forever.

It is now a scheduled job. It runs at 3am on the first. Nobody remembers to run it, because nobody has to.

That person did not disappear. They moved to something that actually needs a person — sitting with branch managers, working out why one location’s food cost drifts and another’s does not. The work that got automated was never the valuable part. It was the tax we paid to see the valuable part.

Multiply that across five companies and the headcount number stops tracking anything real.

Headcount measured what the work cost us. It never measured the work.

What I count instead

Three things, none of them a people number.

  • Decisions per week that genuinely need me. If that is rising, something is wrong with the system, not with the team.
  • Time from question to answer. How long between what is our margin on this dish and having a number I trust. It used to be days. Days is a strategy tax.
  • How much runs without being remembered. Any process that depends on someone remembering to do it is a process that will fail in a bad month.

None of these look impressive on a slide. All of them predict whether the company holds up under pressure.

The part I keep getting wrong

The temptation is to read this as hire fewer people. That is not what I found.

What I found is that the people I do hire have to be different. Fewer hands, more judgment. When the mechanical work goes away, what is left is the part nobody can put in a job description — noticing that something is off, and caring enough to chase it.

That is harder to hire for than capacity. It is also the only thing worth hiring for now.

The unit was never people. It was judgment. We just used to need a lot of people to get a little of it.
