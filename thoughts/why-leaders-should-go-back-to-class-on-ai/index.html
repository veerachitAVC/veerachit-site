Why Leaders Should Go Back to Class on AI

In brief: Executives who learned AI by using it have deep but uneven knowledge. A structured programme, such as the Google AI Professional Certificate, does less to teach the leader than to give the whole organisation a shared floor to build on.

For two years I have used AI every working day. It drafts first versions at Avery & Co, watches stock at Claypot King and assembles reports I once waited a week for. So when I enrolled in the Google AI Professional Certificate, a few colleagues asked, reasonably, why.

The honest answer is that experience had made me confident, and confidence is a poor guide to where one’s gaps are.

The shape of self-taught knowledge

Most senior people learned AI the way I did: by needing it. That is an excellent way to learn what works on your own problems. It is a poor way to learn what you are missing, because you only explore the corners your work happens to pass through.

The result is knowledge that is deep in places and absent in others, and the absences are invisible from the inside. That is what makes them dangerous in a leader, whose assumptions become the organisation’s defaults.

Experience gives you judgment about what to do with a new tool. It does not give you the tool.

What a structured programme adds

Not secrets. Much of the material I recognised. What it provided was structure: the same tools I use daily, laid out end to end across research, writing, analysis and building simple tools of one’s own. Three things proved worth the evenings.

  1. A shared vocabulary. I had private words for things. Now I have the words my managers can look up, which makes every later conversation shorter.
  2. The habits I had skipped. Checking outputs systematically. Being deliberate about which data goes into a tool and which never should.
  3. A floor I can ask others to reach. I cannot hand anyone two years of trial and error. I can hand them a programme.

Credentials versus capability

It would be easy to oversell a certificate. It proves a course was completed. It does not prove that anyone can change how a company runs. That change happens later, inside real systems with messy data and busy people, and it is usually done wrong a few times before it is done right.

But a common foundation makes that work faster. When everyone in the room means the same thing by the same word, the discussion moves on to what actually matters: what to automate, what to leave to people, and where the line between them should sit this quarter.

What leaders should do

  • Take the course yourself before asking your team to. The signal matters as much as the content.
  • Treat it as a floor for managers, not a ceiling for specialists.
  • Follow it with one real project per team, so the vocabulary meets the messy data quickly.

Twenty years in, the easiest trap is believing that experience covers everything new. Being a beginner again, on purpose and in public, is a leadership skill worth keeping.
