The Company of 2040

By Veerachit Jaruthanadech (วีรชิต จารุธนเดช)

In brief: For a century, companies have been organised around the cost of coordinating human work. As capable agents push that cost down, the firm itself is due for redesign. This is a vision, not a forecast: by 2040, the scarce things in a company may be judgement, trust, taste and accountability. Leaders can start building for them now.

Imagine a morning in Bangkok in 2040. A company of forty people serves customers in thirty countries. Overnight, its agents negotiated with suppliers’ agents, answered customers in eleven languages, redrafted a contract and rerouted a shipment around a storm. Nobody was awake. At eight o’clock the chief executive reads one page. It holds three decisions the system declined to make by itself.

This company does not exist. Every piece of it already exists somewhere as a demo or a pilot.

Forecasts like this deserve suspicion. In 1930 John Maynard Keynes expected a fifteen-hour working week within a century. With four years left on that deadline, most managers are still waiting.

The direction, though, can be measured. In 2025 METR reported that the length of task AI agents could finish had been doubling roughly every seven months, while cautioning that the estimate depends on method. Gartner expects at least 15 per cent of day-to-day work decisions to be made autonomously by 2028, up from zero in 2024. Dario Amodei writes of a coming “country of geniuses in a datacenter”. They may all be early. Being early would not change what they point to.

Coase’s question returns

In 1937 Ronald Coase asked why firms exist at all. His answer was that organising work inside a company can be cheaper than the hidden costs of the market: searching, haggling and policing contracts. A firm grows until the overhead of managing it outweighs those savings.

Agents press on both sides of that sum. They can search, negotiate and monitor. They can also lower the overhead of running a large organisation. Nobody can yet say which effect wins. Small firms may do what only giants could. Giants may move like small firms. Either way, the boundary of a company starts to look like a design choice.

Four shifts on the way to 2040

  1. From headcount to capability. Size stops signalling strength. The measure that matters is what a company can reliably decide and deliver.
  2. From org chart to permission map. The most important document in a company will say who and what may act, on what evidence, within which limits. Deloitte finds that close to three-quarters of companies plan to deploy agentic AI within two years, while only 21 per cent of them report a mature model for governing it.
  3. From brand as message to brand as behaviour. When a customer’s agent buys from your agent, nobody reads the advert. Brand becomes what a company reliably does, in a form a machine can check: promises kept, facts stated plainly, behaviour that stays consistent.
  4. From labour as the constraint to energy and trust. The IEA expects data centre electricity use to more than double to around 945 TWh by 2030, slightly more than Japan’s total consumption today. Intelligence is only cheap if someone builds the power. And once anything can be generated, proof of where something came from becomes valuable.

What stays human

Someone still has to answer for the outcome. A customer, a regulator or a court will not accept that the agent decided. Someone also has to choose what is worth doing, which is why taste outlasts execution. The remaining work looks less like production and more like stewardship: setting the purpose, drawing the limits, taking the blame.

The forty-person global company also raises a question no efficiency figure can answer. What happens to everyone else? Keynes saw that this was the harder problem, how people would use their freedom once the economic problem eased. Leaders who ignore it may build something efficient and unwelcome.

What leaders should do

  • Draw your company without the org chart. List the decisions that matter, then who or what makes each one today, and who should make it in five years.
  • Write a permission map for one function: what agents may read, draft, send and change.
  • Turn your brand promises into things that can be checked.
  • Keep a 2040 scenario beside your three-year plan. Test it against the evidence once a year, and include what you owe your people.
  • Keep a human name against every decision that matters.

The company of 2040 will not be defined by how much intelligence it rents. Everyone will rent the same intelligence. It will be defined by what it refuses to delegate.
