The Case for Boring AI

In brief: Most companies start with the AI that makes a good demo. The returns sit somewhere duller: a daily, automatic answer to one question that matters, delivered where people already look, and quiet when there is nothing to say.

Nine o’clock in Bangkok is a particular hour. The traffic on Sukhumvit has peaked, the restaurant shutters are half up, and the kitchens are deciding what they can and cannot serve at lunch.

At that hour a message arrives on my phone. It lists which items are out of stock, or close to it, at each Claypot King branch. Some mornings it runs to four lines. Some mornings it says nothing needs attention. It is the most consequential piece of AI in any of my five companies, and nobody would film it for a conference.

The demo trap

When executives first commission AI, they tend to ask for something that looks like intelligence: a chatbot that answers anything, a dashboard that predicts everything. These are impressive in the boardroom and fragile in the building. They ask people to change where they look, and they rarely say what to do next.

The information in our morning message always existed. It sat in the ERP, accurate and unread, because reading it required someone to remember to open the right screen on a busy day. In a restaurant group, every day is a busy day. We learned about stock-outs the old way: a customer ordered the dish and the kitchen said no.

The most valuable AI does not answer new questions. It answers an old question every day, without anyone having to ask.

Three properties of boring AI that works

Looking back at what stuck and what was quietly abandoned, the survivors share three traits.

  1. It is pushed, not pulled. It arrives in the channel people already open: chat, email, the morning briefing. Anything that requires a new login competes with the job and loses.
  2. It reports exceptions, not status. Our first version listed every low item at every branch. Within a week nobody read it. An alert that fires every day is not an alert; it is wallpaper. Most of the engineering went into subtraction.
  3. Silence carries meaning. When the message says nothing is wrong, people have to be able to believe it. That trust is earned slowly, by being right on quiet days as well as loud ones.

Where the human stays

The agent does not reorder stock, move goods between branches or message suppliers. It could. We chose not to let it, yet.

A branch manager reading almost out of chilli oil knows what the system cannot: that a delivery lands at eleven, or that the dish is off the menu this week. The agent removes the remembering. The deciding stays with the person who can see the kitchen. That boundary will move, but it should move only after months of uneventful mornings have proved the system right.

What leaders should do

  • List the five questions you wish someone answered for you every morning. Pick the one whose answer most often arrives too late.
  • Automate that answer before you commission anything with a chat interface.
  • Measure it by actions taken, not by messages sent. If the message changes nothing for two weeks, it is reporting the wrong thing.

Boring AI will never make a good demo. It will change how the company runs by the second week, and that is a better trade.
